I want to share with you my feelings regarding my ongoing role as a *part-time collaborator* in Computer Sciences, a first-year course

---

# Disclaimer: what's a *part-time collaborator*? 
For those of you who haven't studied in Politecnico di Torino (sadly or not, *depends on your point of view, not mine to judge*), from the 
third year of enrollment and some academical prerequites, students can apply for this *"teaching"* positions in different courses.  
Teaching between quotes because the role of a *part-time collaborators* (from now on, only *collaborator*) is not to replace the Professor 
nor any Teaching Assistant (from now on, *PTA*) in the classroom, but to assist them with their available knowledge.  
I think it's better understood from an example:  
For the Computer Sciences course, the University provides lecture time in Labs to let the students practice, guided by the *PTA*.  
In a lab with 70 students, the single *PTA* cannot help individually all the students that have some doubts.  
The role of collaborators is exactly this: help the students that have doubts to decrease the overall workload for the *PTA*.  
In a few words, if a student has a question concerning their code, other than the *PTA*, he/she/they can also ask us.

# My experience 
As of the moment, I have completed more or less half of the collaboration, so roughly 25 hours.  
I'm confident enough to start thinking and argue some opinions about nowaday's learning and teaching.  

## Thought 1: Students don't ask questions
The most dramatic phenomenon that I've seen so far is the following: students that are struggling **don't ask questions**.  
They are visibly struggling with their problem, can't move on, and instead of asking any collaborator for some help, they just... 
stop working. They ask their favourite AI provider for the solution, they copy paste whatever it answers them, then they put their headphones on, take their phone out
of their pockets (even if usually is already on their desk), and start doomscrolling, or answer text messages, or play videogames, and so on.  
The only way I found to reliably help out students is to take the initiative and ask, *"Do you need help?"*.  
Half of the times, the answer is a straight **no**, even if it's **clear** from a kilometer away that they are struggling.  
My only guess it's that it would hurt their ego.  
Fortunately, in the other half of the times, I do get a question.  
Actually, I don't, but this leads us to the second thought.  
Nevertheless, shout out to the students that ask me first:  
you will go far. 

## Thought 2: Students are becoming non-verbal 
Computer Sciences is an English-taught course. For English-taught Bachelors, English prerequisites are strict, without a Cambridge B2 Certificate (or equivalent) you cannot enroll, full stop.  
This implies that all the students that I get in contact with, have, **at least**, an English knowledge capable of discussing different topics, 
possibly complex, such as their university studies.  
Or, this was my thought before starting my collaboration.  
Because, when I do get an answer from a student, most of the times, what I have is not a clear answer, but just **some mumbled words attached together by the ether**. 
If this wasn't enough, when the words are not mumbled but slightly pronounced, sentences are an unordered set of nouns that look 
more like a tongue twister than a structured sequence of words.  
How can I help a student if I cannot understand what is going on?  
I will sound anachronistic (and a bit boomer), but I think the reason for this is the amount of time spent in front of digital screens.  
Scientific literature agrees that the prolonged used of digital devices delays speech learning (if you don't believe me, as you should, 
a few papers I found confirming what I have just stated: [paper 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7563257/), 
[paper2](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388194657_Exploring_the_effect_of_digital_applications_on_toddlers'_cognitive_and_language_abilities_Frequency_and_duration), 
[paper 3](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/381239102_Effect_of_Gadget_Use_on_the_Development_of_Language_and_Speech_Ability_in_Children_Under_Five)), 
but seeing these effects on **YOUNG ADULTS**, who are completely capable of managing their full cognitive abilites, is just **dystopian**, 
to say the least.  

## Thought 3: Closed mouths don't get fed
In my small area of influence, with the small number of students that are willing to learn and come to university for this 
exact purpose, I do my best do stimulate their neurons to improve in the topic they are learning (*basic Python*).  
Possibly, giving them even other information that I feel it's useful for their future, 
e.g. anything concerning Italian and University bureaucracy, exams they will do, extracurricular possibilities, and so on.     
But to all those students that come in the laboratories just to stare at a monitor, **I won't help you more than I strictly need to**.  
It's something that goes against my own principles and values, as knowledge should neither be restricted by any means nor sold behind a price tag. 
Knowledge is, from my point of view, the only statistical proven way of empowering oneself, this made possible through 
choices, money or network that knowledge can let you reach (again, if you don't trust me, [read this](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/385659047_Empowerment_Through_Knowledge)).  
But, in the moment that someone is **not willing** to put in the effort to acquire such knowledge, and just feels **entitled** to what 
knowledge offers, I don't see any reason to help them out, **as they should be so good to do it themselves**.  

## Thought 4: Not coming to lectures is the easiest way to fail
University feels empowering because it gives you, the student, full control of your time and of your future.  
This freedom comes with the fact that you, **only you**, are to be held **accountable** for your results.  
And University gives you tools to help you succeed in your academic career. Lectures. Professors. Labs. 
Sometimes Tutoring too. Study Groups. Study Rooms. And so on.  
For the majority of students, the majority of their learning has been done in a classroom environment.  
So why not follow the same procedure even in university? Also considering it's the [most-likely way](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877042816309776) to help you pass the exam?  
I just can't wrap my head around the fact that most of the average students think they are above the average, 
and think they already have the skills and knowledge to study full exams by themselves.  
Usually, time will make them learn this the hard way.

# Learning in the AI Era

Heavily influenced by the video linked above, I wanted to give my two cents, to prospective students, on how to learn in the AI era. 

## You **NEED** to struggle
[*No free lunch*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_such_thing_as_a_free_lunch)  
Learning is achieved through **struggle**, commonly stated as *exercise*, and active recall.  
That's pretty much it. You **need** to struggle, understand why you are struggling, then repeat this struggle 
for a different topic, and repeat this loop up until you pass your exam.  
Sometimes you will struggle more, sometimes you won't struggle at all.  
But the more you get used to struggling, the **lighter** each different struggle will feel. 
And sooner or later, studying will feel less like a chore and more like a stimulating activity. 
Without struggle, you won't learn anything.  
Hell, some people get by just like this, memorizing as much as possible hoping it's just enough to pass the exam
without really learning anything. What looks like a shortcut, time and real world experience will reveal 
it was just a **waste of time**.  

## Try first, ask later
The intense use of AI creates a so-called phenomenon of [*"illusion of competence"*](https://www.forbes.com/sites/shannonmckeen/2026/05/18/ai-is-creating-a-dangerous-illusion-of-competence/), where 
users feel like they are learning, while AI is doing the latter struggle instead of the user.  
The solution to this is pretty simple (and remarked multiple times in Prof. Cranmer video): **first try by yourself, then ask AI for the smallest hint possible when you are stuck**.  
Give yourself a chance.  
The human brain has been capable of doing gigantic things, such as making rocks think and communicate between them, send a man **TO THE MOON**, create the Linux kernel, and so on.  
Asking directly for the correct answer to an AI is just a waste of your time and Earth's water. You are not directly learning and the AI is not helping you. 
You are just delegating and postponing your struggle. 
Even better, before asking an AI, if you have the chance to ask a human, (*like collaborators in Computer Sciences Labs, ndr*) do it.
Usually, if not most of the times, humans have more context, more understanding, and more compassion than an AI. 
They also have empathy, which can help you overcome the academic struggle you are facing. They also use less water.      
Is it so tough to believe in yourself and do something on your own before resorting to the comfort of an AI answer?

# Teaching in the AI Era
This next points are more focused towards *PTAs* *(even if I doubt any of them will ever read this)*, but I'd like to describe them as 
*"things that I would try to do if I was in your position"*.

## Problem First, Theory Later
My Mathematical Analysis I Professor *(Prof. Alessandro Audrito, if you will ever read this, you are the GOAT)* used to start every chapter of the course by posing us a problem.  
The solution obviously was to be found inside the chapter itself, which meant that without prior knowledge of the content, we wouldn't be able to solve such problem. 
This asymmetry made us struggle *(recall 2 points above!!)* and gave meaning to the theory we were about to study, as we would now know that for a category of problems, 
this amount of theory would've been applicable.  
So why not extend this method through all courses? And every topic?  
Yes, you are *"wasting"* 5 minutes for the sake of the exercise, but you are gaining something way more valuable: **a reason to learn something**.  

## Projects Rule
Projects are one way of testing the real, tangible knowledge learned in classrooms.  
A practical project either works, or doesn't. If it works, it works well or it works bad. There's no middle ground, and the grade is already in front 
of the examiner. And if a student cheated, it's pretty cheap to find out, asking a few technical questions is more than enough.  
We are studying engineering, practical projects are the closest thing we have with respect to real world jobs.  
**Why are we not doing them?**  
Actually, I do have an answer: classrooms with too many students and not enough *PTAs* to follow them. It's a structural problem.  
It's the reason usually projects are done in the MSc degrees and not in the BSc. 

## Explain your AI use 
You have been learning and teaching for more time than LLMs have ever been capable of writing proper sentences.  
Why don't you explain **how you use AI in your daily life and why**?   
This would give students a **baseline** to compare their AI usage and learning habits with respect to someone who 
is more experienced in the field they are about to study.  
A **nudge** in the direction of ethical and proper AI usage.  
But probably this is a topic worth of another blog post.

# Practical Steps for the lazy students

- Studying and learning are tough. Embrace the struggle. The more you struggle, the lighter it will feel.

- Follow lectures and labs. The chances you are in the average are higher than the chances you are not. 

- Ask Humans first. Human relationships cannot be replaced by AI.

- First do it on your own, and when you are stuck, ask AI for the smallest hint possible.  
Repeat until full understanding is reached.
